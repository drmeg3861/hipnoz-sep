Long before the Games began, many
pessimists predicted issues with
organisation, logistics, and transportation,
some even warning of potential "security
disaster." Yet, none of these problems
materialised, and we had the privilege of
being part of an incredible adventure. What
thwarted these negative predictions was
careful planning, hard work, tenacity,
attention to detail, and a belief in the future,
in humanity, and in our capabilities. Thanks to
everyone who prepared for these Games,
success was achieved—a remarkable lesson
for us all.
When embarking on any venture, there are
always those who focus only on the
negatives, the obstacles, the dangers, and
who share their fears and concerns. And to
some extent, they are right; one should not
plunge head rst into an adventure without
preparation. It's important to take the time to
prepare, to know where you want to go, and
then to gather the necessary resources and
have con dence. But once that is done, it’s
crucial to move forward without hesitation.

Similarly, as healthcare professionals, when a
patient consults us, we must work with them
to determine their goals, paying close
attention to details, their words, and
gestures, and then use all the resources at
our disposal to help them achieve those
goals. Even when situations seem hopeless,
we must not give in to despair, but instead
maintain hope and communicate it to the
patient—a reasonable hope that healing is
possible, or at the very least, that
improvement is within reach.
Of all Milton Erickson's qualities, the most
remarkable and in uential was his ability to
maintain and convey hope to those who
sought his help. Erickson had personally
experienced this hope when, as a young man
a icted by polio, he embarked on a solo
canoe journey for several months. He knew
he could do it, had prepared his journey,
planned his destination and stages, and
disregarded the fears and warnings he
received.
For the ESH, we continue to work on
welcoming new associations, some from
countries that already have hypnosis
institutes, and others that will bring new
countries into the ESH, further solidifying our
position as the leading European association.

entre nosotros. En Francia, acabamos de vivir dos semanas
extraordinarias con los Juegos Olímpicos, que cautivaron al
mundo y demostraron que lo peor no siempre es inevitable.
Mucho antes de que comenzaran los Juegos, muchos pesimistas
predijeron problemas con la organización, la logística y el
transporte, algunos incluso advirtieron de un posible "desastre de
seguridad". Sin embargo, ninguno de estos problemas se
materializó y tuvimos el privilegio de ser parte de una aventura
increíble. Lo que frustró estas predicciones negativas fue la
plani cación cuidadosa, el trabajo duro, la tenacidad, la atención
al detalle y la fe en el futuro, en la humanidad y en nuestras
capacidades. Gracias a todos los que se prepararon para estos
Juegos, se logró el éxito: una lección notable para todos
nosotros. Al embarcarse en cualquier aventura, siempre hay
quienes se centran solo en los aspectos negativos, los
obstáculos, los peligros y comparten sus miedos y
preocupaciones. Y hasta cierto punto, tienen razón: uno no debe
lanzarse de cabeza a una aventura sin preparación. Es importante
tomarse el tiempo para prepararse, saber a dónde se quiere llegar
y luego reunir los recursos necesarios y tener con anza. Pero una
vez hecho eso, es crucial avanzar sin dudarlo. De la misma

consulta, debemos trabajar con él para determinar sus objetivos,
prestando mucha atención a los detalles, a sus palabras y gestos,
y luego utilizar todos los recursos a nuestra disposición para

Trained in hypnosis by Michel Kerouac at the Milton
Erickson Institute of Quebec, I have since adapted this
technique to support elderly patients with dementia in
hospitals and care facilities. This approach has proven
particularly e ective in alleviating anxiety and creating a
more serene environment for care. By implementing
hypnotic tools, we can signi cantly reduce distress in
patients with dementia, enabling caregivers to provide
treatment under much calmer conditions. This method
requires careful adjustment to the speci c needs of
each individual, as patients may exhibit various
behavioural disorders. The positive impact extends
beyond the patients themselves, notably improving the
experience for both caregivers and family members. To
share these insights and o er practical solutions, I co
authored a book with Serge Sirvain, published by
Dunod, which provides guidance on applying this
method in various care settings.
Hypnosis and the elderly
Various studies have demonstrated the e ectiveness of
the implementation of conversational hypnosis among
seniors (Nightingale and Du 2007. Wawrziczny et al.,
2021, Bertoni 2020). This approach can be taught
through short training courses that focus on:
The “pacing” technique, which connects to the person
by meeting them where they are. This involves peaceful
breathing, a reassuring posture, and a hypnogenic
monotone voice. The “sprinkling” of soothing words to
create a sense of security, (e.g., “peacefully”, “quietly”,
“calmly”, “gently” …).

Hypnotic Tool: setting up a “Secure-Photography”
intervention
Due to neurodegenerative pathologies, many people

interests. This research occurs naturally during initial
encounters when the person arrives in the unit. During
the initial set-up of the tool, the professionals of the unit
distribute this work among themselves and posters of
relevant information can be produced in a short time.
Observation of the bedroom provides relevant
information: Are there photos displayed other than
those of grandchildren whom the elderly person may no
longer recognize? Family photos should be be used
cautiously, as nurses may not know the identity of each

Research Reflections
Burkhard Peter (2024) has published a very
interesting paper that he has titled Hypnosis in
psychotherapy, psychosomatics and medicine. A
brief overview. I’m not sure that the title does this
article justice. It is effectively a history of hypnosis,
but not one of those boring, ‘yet another one’
accounts! This one assesses the theories and
practices in the context of the prevailing
philosophies of the time and also evaluates them
against more modern ideas. At 12 pages (not
counting the copious references) it is not, as the title
suggests, particularly brief, but it is a rewarding
read. Rather than attempt a summary I will just
follow through one of the issues it raised for me.
Burkhard makes a distinction between the clinical
use of hypnosis and its scientific investigation,
although he does recommend more research
studies (science) to evaluate the use of hypnosis in
the medical field. The science/therapy distinction is
very much tied to the issue of susceptibility.
Therapists generally have neither the time nor, to be
honest, the inclination to evaluate a patient’s
hypnotisability. I have mentioned in these pages
before that it is striking that so many clinicians have
the impression that their clients don’t seem to
include the non-susceptible. Where have the ‘lows’
gone? Is it that the scientists (who do see hypnotic
lows) are actually working with a different process?
This seems to me to be very unlikely. A more

By
Peter Naish
plausible explanation, I think, is that the anxiety
involved (especially in a discipline such as dentistry)
assists hypnosis by causing a shift in activity
towards the right hemisphere (e.g. Abrahamsen &
Naish, 2021).
Burkhard himself comments upon the weak impact
of hypnotic susceptibility upon therapeutic success
rates, pointing out that many studies have used
relatively few patients, an insufficient number to
demonstrate strong statistical significance. This is
true, but one could argue the reverse. In other
words, if it takes a large number of people to
demonstrate the facilitating effect of having higher
hypnotic susceptibility, then perhaps this means
that the advantage is very small – which is exactly
what the studies show. There appear to be two
possible explanations: either there is no need to be
very hypnotisable to gain therapeutic benefit, or the
presence of people of lower susceptibility, who,
through hemispheric shifts have temporarily
become rather higher, means that there is no longer
a clear spread of susceptibility. This is in contrast to
a scientist testing in a laboratory, where there is no
hemispheric shift to blur the distinction.
It would be nice if it were possible to have a simple
and quick way of enhancing people’s susceptibility,
and thereby produce a clearer, stronger link with
favourable outcomes. With this in mind I was

interested to find a paper by David White and
colleagues (White, Ciorciari, Carbis & Liley; 2009) an
Australian group. They used virtual reality (VR)
headsets with their participants, which delivered
natural sounds and images (e.g. of rivers) while a
spoken induction and suggestions were being
given. In principle this sounds helpful, because it
might assist those who struggle to generate rich
imagery of their own. The authors believed that the
VR was likely to boost the responsiveness of the
lower scorers.
They do not mention the spread of hypnotisability
across participants, but they do say that, rather
than preselecting highs and lows, they merely split
the participants into two, about the mid-range
score, then compared the higher- and lower-scoring
groups Unfortunately, most of the paper reports the
impact of this hypnosis upon the EEG signals
picked up by scalp electrodes, rather than exploring
the specific contribution of the VR. There was a
clear effect of hypnosis, showing that the VR
technique worked, but not whether it was better
than traditional hypnosis.
We do know that VR alone can be used
therapeutically. For example some success has
been found in using immersive VR to treat soldiers
suffering from PTSD. This form of VR presents a
360° world and allows movement. In that way a
soldier can feel that he is advancing (in reality he is
walking on a treadmill) into battle, with all the
expected explosions etc.
However, hypnosis can be used for PTSD too, and it
can generate all the same realistic effects (via

hallucinations) as VR, but without the expensive
equipment. What we have lacked is a study linking
the two. There is a paper which reviews studies to
date, in press in the IJCEH, by Mélanie Louras and
a large team of collaborators. The paper does not
confine itself to hypnosis, but explores the
combination of VR with a range of ‘mind-body’
therapies, i.e. mindfulness, relaxation, meditation
and hypnosis.
Curiously, hypnosis appears to be the only therapy
that is not enhanced by the addition of VR. Perhaps
this is because it is the only therapy that can
generate its own virtual reality.
